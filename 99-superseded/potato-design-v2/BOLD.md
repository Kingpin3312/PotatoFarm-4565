# Bold amber — three options

You were right that the hex was wrong. It was muted because I was
protecting contrast, and protecting contrast on a white page is exactly
what kills a warm colour.

## The constraint, measured

Every bold amber against a near-white ground:

| Colour | Hex | On white | AA text? | On dark |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Signal orange | `#EA580C` | 3.41:1 | **fail** | 5.34:1 |
| Marigold | `#D97706` | 3.06:1 | **fail** | 5.96:1 |
| Saffron | `#E8A33D` | 2.07:1 | **fail** | 8.81:1 |
| Ember | `#DC4E1E` | 3.91:1 | **fail** | 4.66:1 |
| Old gold | `#C99700` | 2.55:1 | **fail** | 7.16:1 |
| Burnt orange | `#C2410C` | 4.97:1 | pass | 3.67:1 |
| What you saw | `#B45309` | 4.82:1 | pass | 3.78:1 |

**Warm colours are light. That is what makes them warm.** Bold amber and
4.5:1 on a white page are close to mutually exclusive — the two that pass
are the two that have been darkened until they stop being bold, which is
precisely what you did not like.

Every one of them passes comfortably on dark.

**So the answer is not a better amber. It is a different page.**

---

## The move

The site flips. Ground is near-black, the accent gets to be the colour it
actually is, and the light sections become punctuation rather than the
base.

This is also the more confident position. **A dark site is a decision. A
white site with a careful accent is the absence of one.** You said you
want bold; this is what bold costs and what it buys.

One consequence, handled: the light punctuation sections use a darkened
step of the same colour, because the bold one fails there. Same brand,
one step down, declared per option rather than fudged.

Every pairing in all three measures AA or better. All of them.

---

## Option D — Signal orange `#F97316`

The loudest thing that is still a brand rather than a warning. Pure
attention. On black it is genuinely striking and it will be the first
thing anybody remembers about you.

**Accent on ground: 6.88:1.**

Closest to a warning colour of the three. Watch it near anything that
means "stop" in the product.

## Option E — Saffron `#F0B23F`

Gold rather than orange. Warm, expensive, and quietly appropriate to the
Gulf without being kitsch — gold reads as value here in a way it does not
in London.

**Accent on ground: 10.23:1.** By far the strongest, which means it holds
in daylight on a phone.

The most premium of the three and the least aggressive. If the worry is
that bold means shouty, this is bold without shouting.

## Option F — Ember `#F4531F`

Orange pulled toward red. Heat rather than light. More urgent than
signal orange and more human than either — it is the colour of something
burning rather than something switched on.

**Accent on ground: 5.6:1.**

The most emotive and the riskiest. It carries some of red's urgency,
which is the thing we removed for good reasons.

---

## What I would take

**Saffron.**

It is the boldest of the three where it counts — 10.23:1 on the ground,
double the others — and it is the only one that reads as *valuable*
rather than as *loud*. In a market where your buyer is handling millions
of dirhams a day, that distinction is worth more than volume.

It is also the only one of the three with no argument against it. Signal
orange sits too near a warning state for a product that has real warning
states. Ember borrows back the urgency we deliberately removed when we
dropped red.

**Signal orange is the answer if the strategy is pure recall.** It is the
one that gets described. Saffron is the one that gets respected while
still being remembered — which for a product built on compliance and
trust is, I think, the better trade.

Look at all three on your phone before you decide. On a screen in
daylight these separate far more than they do here.
