# Three options

Both are complete homepages, built from **one template** so the only
variable is colour. If I had written them separately I would have
unconsciously flattered whichever I built second, and the comparison
would be worthless.

Open both on your phone, in daylight, one after the other. That is the
context that matters — not a laptop in an office.

---

## Option A — Deep teal

**The pitch:** careful with money.

Blue's seriousness with green's reassurance and neither one's baggage.
The family European fintech settled into for a reason — it is what
software looks like when it is holding something that matters.

**What it does for you in a meeting:** nothing is questioned. A
compliance-minded owner looks at it and moves straight to the product. It
sits far enough from Bayut's green never to be mistaken for a portal, and
far enough from the standard SaaS blue to not disappear.

**What it costs you:** it will not be described to anybody. It is
respected, not remembered.

**Accent on ground: 5.94:1.** Comfortable everywhere, including on the
inverted sections, which is where accents usually break.

---

## Option B — Amber

**The pitch:** the one that is awake.

The only palette here that feels like a person rather than a system,
which matters when the whole product is about sounding human at eleven at
night. Amber is lamplight — switched on while everything else is dark.
That is almost embarrassingly on-brief.

**What it does for you in a meeting:** it is remembered. In a category
where every competitor is blue or grey, a brokerage owner describing you
to a partner three days later has something to describe.

**What it costs you:** warm accents fight photographs of property, and
you will be putting a lot of those on the page. It also demands more
discipline — amber done carelessly looks like a discount airline.

**Accent on ground: 4.82:1.** Passes AA with less headroom than teal.
Fine as specified; do not lighten it.

---

## Option C — Midnight blue

**The pitch:** the hour it works.

Your entire proposition is what happens at eleven at night. This is the
only palette that says that without a word of copy.

**The structural thing the other two cannot do.** With midnight, the
accent and the dark sections are the same family — so the inverted
sections stop being "a dark break" and become **the brand**. Teal cannot
do this (a teal section looks like a poster) and amber certainly cannot
(an amber section is unreadable). It is the only option here where the
page has a colour rather than an accent.

One consequence, handled: on a midnight section the accent button would
disappear into the background — measured at 1.61:1. So it inverts to
near-white with a dark label, which is what Apple does in the same
situation. Declared per palette, not assumed.

**What it does for you in a meeting:** it reads institutional. Older,
larger, more established than you are. For a company with no customers
yet pitching a brokerage owner their client list, borrowing a little
gravity is not dishonest — it is the same reason you wear a decent shirt.

**What it costs you:** it is the most conservative of the three. Nobody
has ever been excited by navy. And it is the closest of the three to
looking like everything else in B2B software, though the depth pulls it
away from the standard SaaS blue.

**Accent on ground: 10.39:1.** The strongest of the three by a distance,
which means it holds up in sunlight on a phone — genuinely relevant here.

---

## The honest difference

These are not three shades of one decision. They are three strategies.

**Teal is the choice if you expect to win on trust.** Compliance, the
AML work, the audit trail, the security page — that is your strongest
material, and teal makes all of it feel more true.

**Amber is the choice if you expect to win on being noticed.** You are
launching into a market with an incumbent everyone knows and a
better-funded competitor already live. Being memorable is not vanity in
that position; it is how a small company gets a second meeting.

**Midnight is the choice if you expect to win on looking established.**
It is the one that makes a four-person company look like a fifteen-year
company, and it is the only one where the dark sections carry the brand
rather than just breaking up the page.

---

## What I would do

Midnight changes my answer.

**Take midnight blue.** I recommended teal before it existed and I am
revising, which is worth saying plainly rather than quietly.

Three reasons, and the third is the one that decides it:

**One.** It does the same job teal does — reads as careful, serious,
trustworthy — and does it harder. 10.39:1 against teal's 5.94:1 is not a
technicality; it is why it holds on a phone in Dubai sunlight, which is
where your buyer will actually open it.

**Two.** It is on-brief in a way neither of the others manages. Your
whole product is the hour after everyone goes home. Midnight is that
hour. You will never have to explain the connection.

**Three, and this is the real one.** It is the only palette where the
inverted sections become the brand rather than just a dark break. That
gives the page a colour instead of an accent — and it means every
screenshot, every deck slide and every LinkedIn post carries the brand
even when there is no button in frame. Teal and amber cannot do that.

**Amber remains the answer if the strategy is to be the loud one.** It
is still the only palette a brokerage owner would describe to a partner
three days later. If you decide being talked about matters more than
looking established, take it and commit — do not hedge.

**The one thing worse than any of these is picking one and then asking
whether it could be a bit more like another.** Half of any of these reads
as none of them.
